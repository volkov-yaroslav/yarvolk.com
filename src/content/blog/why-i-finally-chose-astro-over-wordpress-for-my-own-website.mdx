For most of my life on the web, WordPress was not just a tool I used. It was the answer before I had even finished asking the question.

Client site? WordPress.

Personal experiment? WordPress.

Business idea, landing page, content site, small store, weird side project I wanted to test at 2 a.m.? Also WordPress.

So this website matters to me more than it probably should on paper, because **yarvolk.com is the first personal project in my life where I consciously chose something else**.

I built it with Astro, and I have to be honest: I have not felt this relaxed inside my own website stack in a very long time.

## This is not a breakup post

I am not writing this to take a cheap shot at WordPress. That would be dishonest.

WordPress gave me a lot. It taught me how websites behave once they leave the design file and become real systems. It taught me theme structure, content modeling, plugin ecosystems, hosting headaches, performance tradeoffs, and how much work it takes to make a website feel smooth.

It also helped shape projects that genuinely changed my life. [Trimnest](/portfolio/trimnest), for example, is my longest-running personal project and one of the clearest proofs that WordPress can still be incredibly powerful when you need a practical business foundation.

This post is really about something smaller and more personal than that.

For the first time, WordPress was not the automatic answer.

## The default finally started feeling heavy

When you know a platform deeply, its friction becomes invisible. You stop noticing how many layers sit between you and the final result because you have learned how to move around them quickly.

That was me with WordPress.

I knew the admin. I knew the plugin logic. I knew the theme conventions. I knew how to fix things when the stack got weird. But somewhere along the way, I realized I was spending too much energy maintaining the *system around the content* instead of enjoying the content and the code themselves.

For a personal website, I do not really need:

- a database
- a heavy admin layer
- media living in a separate mental model
- plugin decisions for every small feature
- extra abstraction between writing and publishing

What I wanted instead was something direct.

## Astro felt direct in a way WordPress no longer did

That is the word I keep coming back to: **direct**.

With Astro, the project feels close to the source. Content sits in files. Routes are obvious. Components are right there. Layouts are right there. If I want to change the structure of a page, I can open the file and change the page.

There is something weirdly calming about that.

This is roughly what the site looks like from the inside:

```text
src/
├── components/
├── content/
│   ├── blog/
│   │   ├── a-spectacular-rainbow-experience-in-poland.mdx
│   │   └── why-i-finally-chose-astro-over-wordpress-for-my-own-website.mdx
│   ├── pages/
│   └── portfolio/
├── layouts/
├── lib/
└── pages/
    ├── blog/
    │   ├── [...slug].astro
    │   └── index.astro
    ├── portfolio/
    └── index.astro
```

That structure tells me more, faster, than a dashboard ever could.

## The project is easier to reason about

One of the nicest surprises is how little mystery there is.

The homepage pulls content in a way that is easy to read even months later:

```ts
const blogpage = await getPage('pages', 'blog');
const allBlogs = await getPageCollection("blog");
const blogs = allBlogs
  .sort((a, b) => new Date(b.data.date).valueOf() - new Date(a.data.date).valueOf())
  .slice(0, 3);
```

That is such a small thing, but I love what it represents.

No plugin panel.
No custom query hidden in a builder UI.
No guessing where a homepage section is assembled.

Just a file, a collection, and the logic sitting in front of me.

The content model is equally readable:

```ts
const blogCollection = defineCollection({
  type: 'content',
  schema: z.object({
    title: z.string(),
    description: z.string(),
    image: z.string().optional(),
    date: z.string(),
    category: z.string(),
  }),
});
```

Again, it is not about novelty. It is about clarity.

## AI made the tradeoff even more obvious

This part has become impossible for me to ignore.

The more I work with AI inside real projects, the more I appreciate setups where the full context is already visible in the codebase. Astro fits that instinct much better than WordPress does for a personal site.

When content lives in MDX files, components live in `src/components`, and page logic lives in `src/pages`, it becomes much easier to write, edit, refactor, and extend with AI assistance. The project is legible. The patterns are exposed. The agent does not have to guess how your site is stitched together behind a CMS interface.

That does not make WordPress obsolete. It just makes Astro feel better aligned with how I personally want to work now.

## WordPress still wins in plenty of cases

I would still choose WordPress for many projects, especially when the site needs:

- a familiar admin for clients
- a stronger editorial workflow
- plugin-driven features with little custom engineering
- multiple contributors working from a CMS first

But none of those were the main priority for this website.

This site is for me. It is my space to write, show work, tweak things, and iterate quickly. In that context, the lighter option ended up being the better option.

## What I like most is the feeling

It is hard to reduce this to a checklist, because what I enjoy most is not just speed or file structure or the fact that the site is static.

It is the feeling that the stack gets out of my way.

I open the repo and understand it.
I add a post and it feels simple.
I make a design change and it stays local to the code.
I do not feel like I am negotiating with a platform.

That has made me want to write more, ship more, and care more about the site again.

And honestly, that alone makes the move worth it.

## Final thought

For years, I kept choosing WordPress for my own projects because it was familiar, capable, and proven. All of that was true.

But familiarity can quietly turn into inertia.

This site was the first time I stopped and asked a different question: not *what do I already know best?*, but *what would actually feel best for this project?*

For `yarvolk.com`, the answer turned out to be Astro.

Not because WordPress failed me.
Not because Astro is trendy.
Not because I suddenly think every personal site should be rebuilt from scratch.

Just because for the first time in a long time, the simpler option also felt like the more honest one.
