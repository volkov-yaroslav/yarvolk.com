For a long time, WordPress was not just one of my tools. It was *the* tool.

It powered client projects, experiments, business ideas, and most of the websites I built for myself. At some point it stopped feeling like a choice and started feeling like the obvious default. If I was building something personal, I reached for WordPress almost automatically.

That is why this website feels important to me.

**yarvolk.com is the first personal project in my life where I consciously chose something else.** I built it with Astro, and I genuinely could not be happier with that decision.

This is not a post about WordPress being bad. I still think WordPress is an excellent fit for a huge number of websites. It gave me a career, taught me how the web works in practice, and helped me build projects that mattered to me personally. One of the best examples is [Trimnest](/portfolio/trimnest), my long-running e-commerce project built with WordPress and WooCommerce.

But for this website, I wanted something different.

## WordPress had become my default

One of the strange things about working with a platform for years is that you stop questioning it. You know how to move fast in it. You know which plugins solve which problems. You know where the friction is, and you also know how to tolerate it.

That was me with WordPress.

Even when parts of the experience felt heavier than they needed to be, I already knew the workarounds. I knew how to make it bend. So instead of asking whether it was still the right foundation, I kept asking how to make it fit one more project.

For client work, that can still make a lot of sense. WordPress has a mature ecosystem, a familiar admin, and a publishing workflow that many people understand immediately.

But a personal website is different.

My own site does not need a big editorial backend. It does not need a plugin stack just to feel complete. It does not need layers of abstraction between me and the final page. What I wanted was a site that feels close to the code, easy to shape, and pleasant to maintain.

## What changed for me

The biggest shift was not even visual. It was mental.

Over the last couple of years, I started enjoying workflows where content, structure, and styling live close together. I like opening a project and seeing the whole thing in front of me. I like knowing where the text lives, where a component lives, and how a page is assembled without jumping through a dashboard, a media library, plugin settings, and custom fields.

That becomes even more noticeable when AI enters the workflow.

With a code-first setup, the project is much easier to reason about. The content is in files. The layouts are in files. The patterns are visible. If I want help rewriting a section, restructuring a page, or introducing a reusable component, the full context is already there.

In WordPress, a lot of that context is spread across the database, the theme, the plugins, and the admin interface. It works, but it is not nearly as direct.

I think that was the moment when something clicked for me: for a personal website, I no longer wanted the convenience of a CMS more than I wanted clarity.

## Why Astro felt right

Astro landed at exactly the right time.

It gave me the simplicity I wanted without making the project feel overly engineered. The pages are fast. The content model is easy to understand. Writing in MDX feels natural. Components stay available when I need them, but they do not take over the whole experience.

Most importantly, the site feels calm.

There is no sense that I am constantly managing a system around the content. I am just building pages.

That matters more to me than it used to.

With Astro, I can keep the site lean, write posts in a format I actually enjoy, and make design changes without feeling like I am negotiating with a theme architecture. The result is a website that feels much closer to how I think.

It is also simply fun. That part matters too.

## This was not a rejection of WordPress

If anything, choosing Astro made me appreciate WordPress more clearly for what it is actually good at.

WordPress is still the better choice in many situations, especially when the project depends on an admin-driven publishing flow, multiple contributors, client handoff, or a plugin ecosystem that solves real business needs quickly.

I did not move away from it because it failed me.

I moved away from it because, for once, I gave myself permission to choose based on what *this* project needed instead of what I already knew best.

That is an important distinction.

There is a big difference between outgrowing a default and outgrowing a tool. I have not outgrown WordPress. I just do not need it for everything anymore.

## Why this personal site feels better

What makes me happiest is not one specific feature. It is the overall feeling of the project.

The site is fast. It is lightweight. It is easier to maintain. It feels more transparent. I can open the repository and understand it immediately. I can write, tweak, and publish without carrying the overhead that usually comes with a traditional CMS setup.

More than that, it feels like *mine*.

Not in the sense that WordPress sites are not mine, but in the sense that this one feels unusually direct. There is less between the idea and the output. Less ceremony. Less hidden complexity. Less maintenance just to preserve the baseline.

That has made me more excited to keep improving it.

## Final thought

For years, I built personal projects on WordPress because it was the platform I trusted most and knew most deeply. It earned that trust.

But this is the first time I chose something else on purpose, and it reminded me that familiarity is not always the same thing as fit.

Astro turned out to be a better fit for the kind of personal website I wanted to have now: simple, fast, flexible, and close to the code.

I am sure I will keep building WordPress websites. That story is far from over.

Still, I think this project marks a small shift for me. Not because I abandoned WordPress, but because for the first time, I did not treat it as the automatic answer.

And honestly, that has been refreshing.
